Mark glossary

Every mark you'll meet in Dotty: what it's called, what it does and where in the world it's used. Rare marks stay hidden until you meet them in a bonus round.

Accents

Marks that sit on or under a letter and change how it's said. Dictionaries call them diacritics.

  • é

    Acute

    Leans to the right. Used in French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Czech, Hungarian, Polish, Icelandic and more. In French it changes the sound of e, as in café; in Spanish it shows which syllable to stress.

    In Dotty: café, résumé, Málaga

  • è

    Grave

    Say it to rhyme with 'halve'.

    Leans to the left. Found mostly in French and Italian. In French it tells où (where) apart from ou (or).

    In Dotty: crème, première, à la carte

  • ê

    Circumflex

    Nicknamed the little hat.

    Common in French, Portuguese and Welsh. In French it often marks a lost s: château was once chasteau, and fête was feste, like our feast.

    In Dotty: château, crêpe, Ynys Môn

  • ñ

    Tilde

    The little wave. Best known from Spanish ñ, as in señor, which began as a double n with the second one shrunk on top. Portuguese uses it on vowels, as in São Paulo.

    In Dotty: señor, piña colada, São Paulo

  • ë

    Diaeresis

    Also called an umlaut, depending on the job.

    The same two dots do two jobs. In German, Swedish, Finnish, Turkish and Hungarian they change a vowel's sound, and German calls them an umlaut. In French and English they tell you to say a vowel separately, as in naïve and Zoë. Heavy metal bands add them just for show.

    In Dotty: naïve, Zoë, Motörhead

  • å

    Ring

    Also called a kroužek in Czech.

    A small circle on top. Used in Swedish, Danish and Norwegian, where å is a letter of its own near the end of the alphabet, and in Czech as ů.

    In Dotty: smörgåsbord

  • ç

    Cedilla

    From the Spanish for 'little z'.

    The little tail underneath. Used in French and Portuguese to make c soft, like an s, and in Turkish for ç and ş.

    In Dotty: façade, garçon, Besançon

  • č

    Caron

    Also called a háček, Czech for 'little hook', or a wedge.

    A small v on top. Used in Czech, Slovak, Slovenian, Croatian, Latvian and Lithuanian. It usually turns c into ch, s into sh and z into the sound in 'measure'.

    In Dotty: Škoda, Dvořák, Plzeň

  • ă

    Breve

    From the Latin for 'short'.

    A small rounded cup on top. Used in Romanian (ă), Turkish (ğ) and Vietnamese, and in old poetry books to mark a short syllable.

  • ā

    Macron

    From the Greek for 'long'.

    A flat bar on top that marks a long vowel. Used in Māori, Latvian and Hawaiian, and in Latin textbooks.

    In Dotty: Māori

Punctuation

Marks that sit between words and tell you how to read them.

Sentence punctuation

  • .

    Full stop

    Called a period in the US.

    Ends a sentence. Americans also put one after Mr, Mrs and Dr, which is why some official titles have it.

    In Dotty: Dr. No, will.i.am

  • !

    Exclamation mark

    Called an exclamation point in the US.

    Shows surprise, excitement or a raised voice. Some place names and titles carry one as part of the name.

    In Dotty: Westward Ho!, Oklahoma!, Wham!

  • ?

    Question mark

    Ends a question. Greek uses a mark that looks like our semicolon instead.

    In Dotty: Where's Wally?, What's Up, Doc?

  • …

    Ellipsis

    Also called dot dot dot.

    Three dots that show words left out, or a thought trailing off…

  • ¡ ¿

    Inverted exclamation and question marks

    Spanish opens exclamations and questions with upside-down marks, so you know the tone from the very first word.

    In Dotty: ¡Hola!, ¿Qué pasa?

Commas and separators

  • ,

    Comma

    Marks a short pause and separates parts of a sentence. Sometimes it changes the meaning completely. In much of Europe it's also the decimal point, so 3.5 is written 3,5.

    In Dotty: Hello, Dolly!, Let's eat, Grandma!

  • ;

    Semicolon

    Joins two closely linked sentences; stronger than a comma, softer than a full stop. In Greek the same shape is the question mark.

  • :

    Colon

    Introduces a list, a quote or an explanation. In film titles it often splits the name from the subtitle.

    In Dotty: Mission: Impossible

Apostrophes

  • ’

    Apostrophe

    Marks missing letters, as in o'clock (of the clock), and shows ownership. French uses it too: c'est, l'eau.

    In Dotty: o'clock, King's Lynn, Guns N' Roses

Quotation marks

  • “ ”

    Quotation marks

    Also called inverted commas or speech marks.

    Show speech, quotes and nicknames. English uses curly ones. French, Russian and Italian use guillemets instead, and German often uses „ “.

    In Dotty: "Weird Al" Yankovic, Earvin "Magic" Johnson

Brackets

  • ( )

    Round brackets

    Called parentheses in the US.

    Set aside extra information that the sentence could do without.

    In Dotty: (500) Days of Summer, (I Can't Get No) Satisfaction

  • [ ]

    Square brackets

    Show words added by someone other than the writer, often an editor.

    In Dotty: [sic]

  • { }

    Curly brackets

    Also called braces.

    Mostly used in maths, to group a set of things, and in computer code.

  • < >

    Angle brackets

    Also called chevrons.

    Used in maths and computer code, and online for typed hearts.

    In Dotty: <3

Guillemets

  • « »

    Guillemets

    Also called angle quotes. Said to be named after a French printer called Guillaume.

    The quotation marks of French, Russian, Italian, Spanish and many more. French puts a small space inside them: « Bonjour ».

Hyphens and dashes

  • -

    Hyphen

    The shortest line. Joins words together into one idea.

    In Dotty: mother-in-law, Stratford-upon-Avon, Jay-Z

  • –

    En dash

    Roughly as wide as a letter n.

    Links ranges of numbers and dates. A hyphen is often used instead, and Dotty accepts either.

    In Dotty: 1939–1945, 1914–1918

  • —

    Em dash

    Roughly as wide as a letter m.

    Marks a sudden break in a sentence — like this.

Slashes

  • /

    Slash

    Also called an oblique or a stroke.

    Shows alternatives and splits.

    In Dotty: AC/DC, 24/7, and/or

Rare marks

  • ❧

    Fleuron

    Also called a hedera, Latin for ivy.

    A printer's flower, used to decorate the end of a section or a chapter.

    Reversed fleuron

    A fleuron turned the other way, often used in pairs to frame a heading.

  • ¶

    Pilcrow

    The paragraph mark.

    Medieval scribes painted it in red to start a new thought. Word processors still show it where you pressed Enter.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • §

    Section sign

    Points to a numbered section of a law or document, as in §12.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • ☞︎

    Manicule

    Latin for 'little hand'. Also called an index or a fist.

    Readers drew them in margins to point at important lines, centuries before the mouse pointer.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • ⁂

    Asterism

    Three asterisks in a triangle, used to break up a chapter.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • †

    Dagger

    Also called an obelisk.

    The footnote mark that comes after the asterisk.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • ‡

    Double dagger

    The next footnote mark after the dagger.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • ‽

    Interrobang

    A question mark and exclamation mark in one, invented by an American advertising man in 1962 for questions like 'You did what‽'

  • ?

    Percontation point

    A backwards question mark proposed in the 1580s for rhetorical questions. It never caught on.

    Locked. Meet this mark in a Dotty bonus round to reveal it.

  • ÷

    Obelus

    Before it meant divide, it marked doubtful passages in ancient manuscripts.
